## Why

Drivers key the same delivery twice: once on a paper sheet in the van, and again at the depot at the end of the shift. The second entry takes about forty minutes a day per driver, and it is where most of the errors in the delivery records come from: a transposed reference, a signature that cannot be read, a sheet left in the cab.

A tablet in the van removes the second entry. The driver records the delivery once, with the customer's signature on the screen and a photograph of where the parcel was left, and the record reaches the depot system the moment the van has signal. Customers get the confirmation the same day instead of the next.

## The three waves

Wave one is the Nordic fleet, starting in the second quarter. Those vans share one mount and one depot system, and Halvard Logistics, the largest customer the fleet serves, asked for signed proof of delivery first.

Wave two is the rest of Europe in the third quarter, depot by depot, each one starting only when its own team has been trained and has run a week in parallel with paper. Wave three is the leased vans in the fourth quarter. They need a different mount, which is the one part of the plan still waiting on a supplier. No wave starts until the one before it has run for a month with fewer than one tablet fault per hundred vans a week.

## What could go wrong

Connectivity in rural depots is the risk everybody names. The app works offline for a full shift and synchronises when the van is back in range, so a missing signal delays a record rather than losing it.

The other risks are quieter: a driver who keeps the paper sheet as well, a depot that trains late, a mount that rattles loose. Each depot lead reports weekly on how many deliveries were recorded only on the tablet, and that number, not the count of tablets fitted, is how the project measures itself.
